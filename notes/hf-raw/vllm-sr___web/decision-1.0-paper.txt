                                        Decision 1.0
                     Towards Open Decision Foundation Models
                        Xunzhuo Liu, Haichen Zhang, Mahdi Ghodsi, Andy Luo,
                           Adam Kovacs, Huamin Chen, Xue Liu, Bowei He

    Abstract. Intelligent software needs reusable judgments inside its control flow: selecting an
    action, testing a condition, or assessing evidence against a rubric. We present Decision 1.0, an
    open-weight family that turns textual state and runtime-defined questions into typed probability
    distributions. Its Choice, Noul, and Score interface lets applications specify outcomes at inference
    time and compose predictions into agents and workflows. Each question produces its candidate
    distribution without autoregressive text generation. Six models cover bidirectional encoder and
    causal decoder architectures, including a workflow specialist. Training draws on verifiable tasks,
    natural-language annotations, and workflow adaptation. On a common decision benchmark, Lux-
    9B achieves 84.17% on explicit decisions and 91.25% on inference, with a weighted overall score of
    77.22%. Results also expose gaps in composition, transfer, and probability calibration. Released
    weights and inference code provide a basis for developing decision models as reusable components
    of software.

1      Introduction
As learned models become components of software, their outputs must support decisions inside pro-
grams. Routing, agent control, and workflow automation require judgments that can be combined
with explicit state, constraints, and rules. A reusable model for this setting should interpret a task
supplied at runtime and return probabilities over the application’s allowed outcomes.
Jev’s System One interface highlights this opportunity through typed, probabilistic decisions [1]. De-
veloping the idea as an open research direction requires access to model weights and implementations:
researchers need to examine how representations and training affect decisions, adapt models to new
domains, and test whether their probabilities remain useful as tasks change.
We introduce Decision 1.0, six open-weight models spanning bidirectional encoders and causal decoders
under a common Choice, Noul, and Score interface. The family includes general decision models and
a workflow specialist, with documented architectures, training sources, and evaluation. Our aim is
to establish an open foundation for programmable decision-making: models that can be adapted
to different applications while preserving an interface that software can compose and inspect. This
release studies the current capabilities of that approach and the remaining challenges in composition,
transfer, and calibration.

3     Model Architectures
Decision 1.0 combines two architectures under one typed interface (Table 1). Kai and Lex use bidi-
rectional encoders; Eos, Sol, Nox, and Lux use causal backbones. Both score candidates supplied
at runtime, but differ in how they encode the evidence and extract candidate representations. Each
checkpoint has independent weights.

The readout uses four independently parameterized projections; only the candidate-side nonlinear

An early candidate endpoint cannot attend to later candidates, but the final query conditions ev-

4     Data and Training
The central training problem is to bind a textual task definition to its allowed outcomes while pre-
serving useful language understanding. Procedural supervision supplies explicit rules, controlled state
changes, and verifiable answers. Natural-language annotations provide reading, intent, inference, and
rubric judgments that templates alone do not cover. The documented Sol, Nox, and Lux mixtures do
not use official Jev predictions as training labels.

4.1    Distribution supervision
In the documented Lex, Sol, Nox, and Lux distribution-supervised stages, a target y ∈ ∆K−1 gives
the objective

with T = 1 during training. Hard labels become one-hot distributions; source soft labels retain their
recorded distribution. Human ordinal means can be represented by interpolation between adjacent
levels, preserving the annotated mean. Candidate reordering must also reorder its targets, and ordered
rubric levels cannot be shuffled without preserving their semantics.

4.2    Encoder adaptation and workflow specialization
Kai inherits multilingual supervision spanning inference, intent, evidence, and human ratings. Repre-
sentative sources include SNLI, MASSIVE, BoolQ, TyDi QA, HelpSteer2, and OpenAssistant [9, 16,



                                                        6
17, 19–21]. Their annotations are converted into candidate decisions with supporting context where
required.
Kai adapts Choice and Score paths on approximately 21,000 complete-input examples with frozen
shared embeddings, retaining an earlier Noul path in the released checkpoint. Lex specializes an earlier
Kai checkpoint on 6,000 generated training decisions from typed-decisions [6], covering customer
service, invoices, security incidents, and agent traces. It combines teacher-derived soft targets and
hard labels equally, adapts all three paths with frozen embeddings, and follows a fixed refit schedule
before evaluation on the 2,000-decision test set.

4.3    Causal adaptation and retention
Sol and Nox use an English/Chinese curriculum of procedural decisions, complemented by intent and
inference annotations [22–24]. Replay retains earlier task coverage. Natural-language adaptation uses
a 24,000-example mixture of retained decisions, Cosmos QA, SQuAD 2.0 answerability, and SNLI [7–
9]. Examples from each source-parent or detected near-duplicate group are assigned to the same
training, selection, or calibration split. Known evaluation matches are excluded from custom training
construction.
Sol uses candidate cross-entropy training. Nox adds supervised retention through frozen vocabulary
rows for legal answer letters, with this auxiliary readout removed at deployment. Lux adapts Qwen3.5-
9B on a 24,000-example mixture of retained decisions and human-annotated judgments. Its released
final stage uses hard-label cross-entropy and replaces eligible opaque option keys with semantic de-
scriptions while preserving the evidence, candidate descriptions, order, and labels.
Eos uses head-only warmup followed by full-model adaptation over 24,000 training rows. Differences
in initialization and training recipes make the family a comparison of released systems rather than a
controlled scaling or attention-direction study.

4.4    Selection and calibration
Causal checkpoint selection emphasizes decision accuracy, with probability quality used to break ties
where documented. A positive scalar temperature is fitted separately by minimizing negative log
likelihood [10], adjusting distribution sharpness while preserving the argmax. Lex uses a fixed final
schedule and no test-based temperature fitting.


5     Evaluation
We evaluate general decision quality, workflow specialization, and probability quality separately. The
common benchmark compares five general Decision models with ten reference systems; Lex is assessed
on its dedicated workflow test. All results use the fixed public revisions in Appendix A.

5.1    Setup and protocol
The common benchmark contains 3,766 scored decisions across 54 task slices. Five panels measure
different capabilities: Decisions covers explicit classification, Boolean rules, and rubric judgments;
Composition tests interacting constraints and state updates; Reading tests comprehension of provided
evidence; Inference tests answerability, entailment, and commonsense judgments; and Transfer tests
reuse on the external Kev suite [13]. The suite covers English and Chinese. Appendix C gives the
source datasets, sample counts, and aggregation rules.
Systems receive the same examples and candidate order through their native inference interfaces. We
compare the official Jev 1.13.0 service, English and multilingual Laya, Decider, three Kev sizes, and

untuned Qwen3.5 references [1, 5, 11–13]. Panel scores measure categorical accuracy, including for
Score questions. Because much of the benchmark was observed repeatedly during development, these
results characterize the released systems rather than establish performance on a fresh blind test.

5.2     General decision accuracy
Lux leads Decisions and Inference among the 15 systems in Table 2, at 84.17% and 91.25%, and ranks
second by the published Overall score. Relative to its untuned Qwen3.5-9B reference, these are gains

points over their corresponding references. Eos exceeds Kev-0.8B on the four non-transfer panels, and
Kai exceeds both general Laya models on those panels. These system-level comparisons do not isolate

5.3     Workflow specialization
On the separate typed-decisions test of 2,000 judgments from 400 state groups, Lex reaches 78.15%
accuracy versus 76.60% for the official Laya specialist. The paired group-bootstrap 95% interval
for this 1.55-point difference is [−0.20, +3.15]; the overall advantage is not statistically established.
Lex has higher Score accuracy, whereas Noul accuracy and probability-quality measures favor Laya.
Specialization yields a mixed profile rather than a uniform improvement.

5.4     Probability quality
A useful decision distribution must convey more than its most likely label. Table 3 compares Lux
with the two systems that achieve higher transfer accuracy. Lux is close to Kev-9B in Brier score and
negative log likelihood, but has higher calibration error. Among these three systems, Jev leads all
three measures. Transfer calibration remains a limitation despite temperature fitting.

6    Related Work
Candidate-conditioned classification connects to entailment-based zero-shot classification, where labels
are supplied as descriptions rather than fixed output indices [14]. ModernBERT and mmBERT provide
bidirectional representations; Qwen3.5 provides the causal initialization used here [3–5]. The decision
interface is compatible with either attention direction.
Jev, Laya, Decider, and Kev explore typed probabilistic interfaces and candidate readouts [1, 11–13].
Decision builds on these designs with a released encoder and causal family, multilingual and workflow
adaptation, and a common evaluation of accuracy, transfer, and probability quality. Temperature
scaling addresses calibration separately from classification accuracy [10].


7    Future Directions
Future work targets the cost of multi-question inference while preserving decision quality.

Reuse state computation and improve batching. An identical causal prefix could be computed once
and branched across questions, reusing attention key/value caches together with Gated DeltaNet
recurrent and convolutional states. Length-aware batching and serving methods such as FlashInfer [25]
motivate further study on this hybrid backbone. Evaluation should vary input length, candidate count,
question count, and batch size, measuring throughput and tail latency.

Compress the model while preserving decisions. Quantization methods such as SmoothQuant [26]
motivate lower-precision backbone execution with a higher-precision readout. Distillation of candidate
distributions, including methods such as decoupled knowledge distillation [27], could transfer a larger
model’s behavior to a smaller decision model. Accuracy, calibration, and candidate-order robustness
should guide these studies.

Learn a reusable state representation. Perceiver IO and in-context autoencoding [28, 29] motivate a
compact state representation shared by question-specific readers. This would require a new trained
architecture that preserves evidence needed by questions beyond those used to construct the represen-
tation.

Allocate computation to difficult questions. Routing and cascading can allocate model capacity under
a cost–quality objective [30]. Within-model early exits would additionally require trained intermediate
heads and validated exit confidence. Policies for grouped requests should be assessed on task-held-out
data using per-question quality and end-to-end latency.

8    Limitations and Availability
Decision 1.0 is text-only and relies on supplied evidence. The general evaluation covers English
and Chinese; quality depends on the task, input budget, evidence, and candidate set. Training and
evaluation reuse public task families, and upstream pretraining exposure is unknown. Differences in
recipes and native input handling limit attribution to individual architectural changes.
We release weights, inference implementations, configurations, and evaluation documentation [2]; en-
coder releases also include continued fine-tuning code. Not every assembled training corpus, construc-
tion script, or final optimization setting is distributed. Project artifacts use Apache 2.0 with retained
upstream notices; source datasets retain their own terms.


9    Conclusion
Decision 1.0 maps runtime-defined alternatives directly to typed probabilities through bidirectional or
causal models. The family improves explicit decision performance and supports workflow adaptation,
while composition, transfer, order sensitivity, and uncertainty remain open problems. Released weights
and inference code provide a basis for further research.

C     Evaluation Details
Panels and aggregation. Decisions and Composition contain 880 examples each and macro-average
ten task families. Reading contains 480 BoolQ and English/Chinese Belebele examples [16, 18], as-
signing one half of its weight to BoolQ and one quarter to each Belebele language. Inference equally
averages four 120-example sources: Cosmos QA, SQuAD answerability, SNLI, and supplied-fact QASC.
Transfer uses micro accuracy on the 1,046 answerable examples of the Kev suite. Score accuracy uses
the most probable rubric category, not error in the expected ordinal score.
Overall combines the unrounded panel accuracies with the published weights: 30% Decisions, 25%
Composition, and 15% each for Reading, Inference, and Transfer. The weights were chosen after
observing results and provide a descriptive ranking. Reported differences are also computed before
display rounding.

Inference and coverage. Untuned Qwen uses fixed answer-letter logits without generated-text parsing.
Models retain their native templates and temperatures. Kev uses a BF16 backbone and FP32 head
with date injection disabled, differing from its authors’ FP32 configuration. Laya truncates some
transfer inputs; Jev’s server-side input retention is unobservable. Failures count as incorrect when a
gold accuracy label exists.

Exposure and uncertainty. The external suite is excluded from custom training, checkpoint selection,
and temperature fitting, although observed results can inform later research. Paired bootstrap resam-
pling over source groups preserves related examples; Lex’s separate comparison resamples its 400 state
groups. These intervals quantify example-sampling uncertainty, not benchmark reuse, training-run
variation, or unknown upstream pretraining exposure. Differences between architectures and training
recipes limit attribution to individual components.
